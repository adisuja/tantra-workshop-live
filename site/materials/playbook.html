FoundationsThe thing you're actually building

What is AI RevOps?

RevOps is one word for something most companies run as two departments — and the gap between those two departments is where most revenue quietly dies.

Marketing owns the top. The ads, the content, the posts, the emails you send, the messages you send, the billboard, the spot on TV. Sales owns the bottom. The call, the demo, the proposal, the close. Between them sits a handoff: marketing counts leads, sales counts deals, and nobody owns the distance in between. Ask both teams why a quarter missed and you'll get two confident answers that don't touch each other.

Revenue operations is the decision to stop running it as two jobs. One system, one owner, one set of numbers — from the first second a total stranger sees your name, to the moment money clears your bank account.

Trace the whole thing once and it stops feeling abstract. Someone who has never heard of you sees something: an ad in a feed, a founder's post, a cold email, a message, a billboard on a commute, thirty seconds on TV. That touch has exactly one job — be seen by the right person. They click. They land somewhere. They hand over an email or a number, or they don't. They get a sequence. They turn up to something: a page, a session, a call. They're qualified, or they aren't. They see an offer. They buy. The payment settles. They get onboarded — and then they either stay or they leave.

Cold stranger to cleared payment — one system, not two departments
Read the top row as a set of numbers, not a story. Seven stages, seven counts, six rates between them. The moment you own all of them you stop arguing about whose fault a bad quarter was.

Here's the part that's actually new. Those four layers — touchpoints, people, data, plumbing — have always existed. Every company has had them, in some state, forever. What changed is that all four are now addressable by software that can reason. An agent can read the CRM, write the sequence, watch the step rates, spot which one dropped, draft the fix and schedule the send. Not because the model is magic, but because each layer already exposes an API and something can finally hold all of them at once.

◆ So what does the "AI" in AI RevOps actually mean

Not a chatbot bolted onto a website. It means the operator of the system is an agent rather than a person with twelve browser tabs open. You still decide the strategy, the offer and what ships. The agent does the reading, the writing, the wiring and the measuring — the work that was previously split across a marketer, an ops person and a sales manager who each saw a third of the picture.

FoundationsWhy this shape, and not just a better product

Why do you need a funnel — a conversion engine?

Here's the deal: people buy on a promise and stay on a result. Those are two different forces, and almost every funnel ever built addresses only the first one.

You can be genuinely excellent at earning the yes and still lose the customer — because nothing you built does anything about the gap between the moment they pay and the moment the thing actually works for them. That gap is where churn lives. It is also, almost always, invisible on the dashboard that told you the campaign was a success.

So a conversion engine is not a machine for getting a yes. It's a machine for delivering value continuously — before the sale, to earn the yes, and after it, to make the yes hold.

Excitement peaks at purchase. Value arrives later. The gap is the churn window.

Why the sale alone doesn't hold it

Software is supposed to be stickier than a course. It isn't — not on its own. Russell Brunson puts the mechanism precisely, crediting it to Bill Glazer: what he calls the pain of disconnect is not a property of the software at all. It's a property of what the customer has built inside it. Someone who never deployed anything churns exactly like a course they never opened. What creates the switching cost is usage — and what creates usage is being taught.

Alex Hormozi's SaaS case study is the cleanest version of this. His team replaced a 25-person group onboarding call with a one-to-one session that restated the customer's own goal, delivered a single quick win, and booked the next meeting from inside that meeting so nobody was left in no-man's-land. Ascension to the next offer went from 20% to 48%. His principle is worth writing down: the best way to get someone to expect value in the future is to give them value today.

Dan Martell's version is blunter. His own company ran 12% monthly churn — a nine-month customer. A client of his went from 18% to 4% monthly by building an on-ramp (teach the movements before the first class), a peer community so people feel part of something rather than merely using a tool, visible case studies, and a member-at-risk monitor.

Four different mechanisms — they need four different assets

Why every brand you can name already does this

Look at any well-known software or service brand and you will find the same set of things, without exception. They publish content continuously. They run webinars and live sessions. They write books. They run workshops and events. They host a community.

None of that is charity and none of it is "brand awareness." Every one of those is a carefully built conversion engine — a mechanism that takes someone cold, gives them something genuinely useful, and moves them one step closer to buying. And then keeps running after they've bought, because staying on top of mind is what turns a customer into a member of a tribe. The same asset does two jobs depending on which side of the sale the reader is standing on.

The same five assets, working both sides of the sale
You are not building five things. You're building five wrappers around one engine — and each one keeps earning after the sale, which is the half almost everybody skips.
On the numbers people quote here. A Forrester Consulting study commissioned by Intellum (2024, 300 director-level-and-above decision-makers) reported roughly 22% higher product retention, 38.3% higher adoption and 35% higher lifetime value per trainee. Treat those as directional, not proof: the study was commissioned by a customer-education vendor and the figures are self-reported by the people who run those programmes. A TSIA survey of about 2,800 customers found 68% say they use the product more after training — that is perceived behaviour, not measured usage. TSIA itself notes only 11% of firms have ever analysed whether content consumption actually affects renewal. Use them to argue direction; never as a promised result.
◆ The honest limit — read this before you over-build tutorials

Results alone plateau. Brunson's own position in Expert Secrets is that if the only thing you ever do is help someone achieve a result, they will eventually leave you anyway. Enablement gets them to the result. Community and a visible next rung are what keep them past it. Build both, or you'll do all this work and still watch people go quiet at month nine.

◆ What this means for the next three days

Every page, sequence and automation you build is in service of one thing: moving the value crossing earlier and then continuing to deliver value on the far side of the sale. If a thing you're about to build does neither, it's decoration — cut it.

Where your offers live: the value ladder

Before you pick a funnel type, you need to know what the funnel is selling into. Almost nobody buys the expensive thing first. They buy a small thing, it works, and they climb.

Here's the deal: a funnel isn't a machine for selling one product. It's a machine for moving someone up a ladder. And the ladder has a shape — each rung asks for more commitment and returns more value, while the crowd on each rung gets smaller.

The value ladder — commitment rises, audience narrows, value compounds
The load-bearing rung is the entry offer. Price it so the revenue it produces covers the cost of the traffic that produced it, and your top of funnel becomes free — every rung above it is then pure margin.
◆ Example · this workshop's own ladder

You're standing on one right now. Free content brought you here. The 3-Day AI RevOps Workshop is the entry offer — deliberately priced to cover the cost of filling it. Inside it you get a month of the app included. Above it sits a core program (course, community, group coaching) and above that a flagship tier (long-horizon access plus one-to-one implementation). Five rungs, one system, and each rung sells the next by working.

◆ Pro tip · build the ladder before the funnel

If you only have one thing to sell, your funnel has to do all the persuading in one shot — and your traffic has to be profitable on the very first transaction. That's the hardest game in marketing. Two rungs is dramatically easier than one. If you don't have a second rung yet, write one down today; you'll need it by Day 3.

Most businesses are great at one of these and terrible at the rest. A "viral" post with no capture step leaks every visitor. A beautiful sales page with no traffic is a billboard in the desert. The funnel's job is to make all four work together.

◆ Why retention is the whole game

Acquiring a customer costs money. Keeping one costs almost nothing. That's why the north star of this entire system isn't the first sale — it's MRR: recurring revenue that compounds month after month. Everything before it exists to earn Day 3's retention.

The one equation to memorize

You don't need an MBA. You need this:

The funnel equation

Profit = (Traffic × Conversion rate × Price) − Cost to deliver

Pull any one lever up and profit moves. The magic is that you can pull all four — and a funnel is just the machine that lets you pull them on purpose instead of by accident.

That's the version you can hold in your head. But it has a flaw: "conversion rate" is a guess. On Day 1 you'll replace every symbol in that equation with a number your funnel actually reports — a counted event with a named source — and by Day 3 you'll be reading those numbers weekly and fixing whichever one is lowest.

◆ The rule that runs through the whole week

Never model your funnel with invented rates. Every step you build gets instrumented as an event. The count of that event is the metric. A "conversion rate" is just one count divided by another. If you can't fetch it, you can't fix it — and you'll be optimising by vibes.

Funnel vs. random marketing

Random marketing is post-and-pray. You publish, you boost, you hope. A funnel is different: every step has one job, and each step earns the next. If a visitor doesn't take the next action, that step is broken — and you know exactly which one.

◆ The mindset shift

If a customer doesn't understand or use what you sell, that's not their fault — it's yours. Your funnel owns the whole journey, from "who are you?" to "I can't run my business without this." Own the whole journey and retention stops being a department.

The three questions every funnel answers

  1. Who is this for? Specific enough that the right person reads it and thinks this is me — and the wrong person leaves without wasting your money.
  2. What transformation do they get? Nobody buys a tool. They buy the after-state: the version of their business that exists once the problem is gone.
  3. Why now, and why you? The mechanism. The reason your way works when everything they've already tried hasn't.

The rest of this half is just answering those three properly. Question one is your ICP. Question two and three are your offer. Then you pick the funnel shape that carries them, and you write down the arithmetic that says whether it can work.

Step 4 — Write down the arithmetic

Last thing today. Before you build anything, you write the equation that says whether this can work — and you write it in quantities you will actually be able to fetch, not in conversion rates you made up.

This is the single biggest difference between a funnel that gets optimised and one that gets abandoned. A rate you invented can never be wrong, so it never teaches you anything. A counted event can be wrong tomorrow, which is exactly what makes it useful.

Every term is a counted event with a named source
Today you'll fill this in with holes. That's correct. Anything you can't count yet gets written as unknown — will measure, and Day 3 is where you instrument it so it stops being unknown.
◆ Do this · the honest baseline

Write the equation out with your own numbers. For every term you have real data for, put the number and where it came from. For every term you don't, write "unknown — will measure" and move on. Do not fill a gap with a benchmark you read somewhere. A funnel modelled on borrowed averages will be optimised toward someone else's business.

Task 1.6Set your baseline and your target

Two lines of arithmetic, working backwards:

  1. The revenue you want, per month. A real number.
  2. Divide by your core offer price → buyers needed per month.
  3. Divide by whatever purchase rate you can actually evidence (or mark unknown) → qualified people needed.
  4. Keep going up the chain until you reach visits needed. That number is your traffic target — and it's the number Day 3's second half is built around.

If the visits number is absurd, you've learned something valuable on Day 1 rather than in month three: your price, your offer, or your funnel type has to change.

Funnel vs. campaign — know the difference

CampaignA momentA launch, a promo, a burst. Has a start and an end. Great for spikes, useless for stability.
FunnelA machineAlways on. A stranger can enter today, next week or next year and get the same journey. This is the asset.

Campaigns give you a good week. Funnels give you a predictable business. You'll run campaigns into your funnel — but the funnel is what you own.

◆ Why this distinction costs people money

Most businesses rebuild their messaging every campaign. New launch, new deck, new landing page, new emails — all written from scratch, all slightly different. Three campaigns later nobody can say what the company actually promises. A conversion engine makes that impossible, because every asset is derived from the same block of context rather than re-invented.

Step 2 — Build the diagnose-then-prescribe engine

Here's the deal: the highest-converting thing you can do to a stranger is not pitch them. It's diagnose them — and then prescribe. People don't argue with a conclusion they helped produce.

This mechanic works everywhere. It's a poll inside a live session, a qualification form on a registration page, an opt-in question on a reverse squeeze bridge, or a full scored assessment. Same engine, different wrapper.

Ask → score → band → prescribe (the reusable engine)
The band is the story, the score is the hook. A number gets attention. The band — "you're conversion-limited" — is what makes the recommendation feel inevitable rather than sold.
◆ Example · the same engine in four wrappers

In a live session: three polls, and the poll answers segment your follow-up. On a registration page: two qualification fields that route registrants into different reminder sequences. On a reverse squeeze bridge: one question between Part 1 and Part 2 that decides which Part 2 they get. As a standalone assessment: the full scored quiz with a gauge and a paywall. Same ask → score → band → prescribe. The only thing that changes is how many questions you can get away with.

Task 2.2Design your diagnostic
  1. Name 3–5 score axes. These are the dimensions your offer actually improves. If your offer fixes conversion, one axis is conversion. Don't invent axes you can't help with.
  2. Write 6–12 agreement statements and tag each to an axis. Statements, not questions: "I know which step of my funnel leaks the most" — agree/disagree.

You have a funnel and you have data. This half is the discipline that turns those into a business: know your unit economics, find the single worst step, change one thing, measure, keep or revert. Every week, forever.

◆ Why one leak at a time

Fixing five things at once feels productive and teaches you nothing — if the number moves you can't say which change did it, and if it drops you can't say which to undo. One change per leak is slower per week and vastly faster per quarter.

Step 1 — Know what a customer costs and what they're worth

Two numbers decide whether you have a business or an expensive hobby: what it costs to acquire a customer, and what that customer is worth over their life. If the second is comfortably bigger than the first, you can buy growth. If it isn't, more traffic just loses money faster.

◆ Pro tip · the ratio, not the number

A $600 cost per customer is not "high" or "low" on its own. Against a $400 lifetime value it's a business that dies; against a $4,000 one it's a business you should be pouring money into. Watch the ratio. Roughly 3× is the conventional floor for a healthy funnel, and below about 1× you are paying for the privilege of having customers.

Step 3 — Run the loop every week

Six steps, one lap per week, forever
The loop can run semi-autonomously: the agent fetches both reports on a schedule, flags the worst leak and proposes a variant — you still decide what ships.
And the bottleneck always moves. Fix the worst step and a different one becomes the worst. That's the loop working, and it's why this is a weekly habit, not a project with an end date.
Task 5.1Run one full lap today
  1. Observe — pull both reports from yesterday's traffic.
  2. Locate — name the single worst step out loud. Write it down.
  3. Hypothesise — one sentence: "I think it leaks because ___, so I'll change ___."
  4. Experiment — put the variant in the spec, serve it to half your sessions, keep everything else identical.
  5. Diary it — the change, the date, the metric you expect to move, and what you'll do either way. Future you will not remember.

Step 4 — Move the money to the channel that actually buys

Here's the rule that saves the most money: reallocate toward the lowest cost per buyer, not the lowest cost per click. A cheap click that never converts is the most expensive traffic you own.

Same spend, redistributed by cost per buyer
Figures are a teaching model. The method is not: rank by cost per buyer, fund the top, starve the bottom, keep a small test budget on everything so you'd notice if it changed.
◆ Pro tip · never cut a channel to zero

Leave a small always-on test budget everywhere. Channels change — an audience gets cheaper, a platform changes its algorithm, a competitor stops bidding. If you cut to zero you'll never find out, and you'll be the last to know when the cheapest channel in your market becomes available again.

Step 6 — Where $1M ARR actually comes from

Not from one enormous month. From a wheel that gets slightly easier to turn each time: a new customer joins, gets a fast win, asks for more, gets it, stays, and refers. Every turn adds recurring revenue and lowers the cost of the next customer.

◆ Retention is the only lever that multiplies the others

Doubling your traffic doubles your buyers. Doubling your retention doubles the value of every buyer you have ever acquired and every one you will acquire. That's why the north star here is recurring revenue, not the first sale — and why the 48 hours after someone pays deserve more of your attention than the 48 days before.

◆ What changes per funnel type · scaling
Webinar funnelScale by frequency — run it monthly rather than occasionally. The assets are already built, so each additional run costs mostly promotion. Watch show-up rate as volume grows; it usually dips first.
Workshop · challengeScale by cohort size and cadence, but completion rate is the constraint. A cohort too large to feel personal converts worse on day five — and day five is where the money is.
Reverse squeezeScale by content volume. Each new Part 1 is a permanent new entrance, and old ones keep producing. Slowest to start, best compounding of the three.
Other types: tripwire and free-plus-shipping funnels scale on ad spend, limited only by how fast the upsell recovers acquisition cost · community funnels scale on member-driven referral and stall if the community goes quiet · JV funnels scale by adding partners, so partner recruitment becomes the growth function.

What runs on which day — and the one thing you must start early
The amber bar is the trap. Domain warm-up is the only task with a hard external wait, and it's the one people start last. Begin it on day one and it's ready when your funnel is; begin it on day four and you'll be sitting on a finished funnel with nowhere to send from.
◆ How the daily loop works

Watch the live session · fill the survey in parallel while it's fresh · get your generated output by message and email the same day · do the short homework that unblocks tomorrow. Skipping the homework is the single biggest reason people fall behind, because each day's session assumes yesterday's output exists.
